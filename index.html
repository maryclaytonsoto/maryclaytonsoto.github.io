About

Where Fields Overlap

I'm a biomedical engineering and mathematics double major at Duke's Pratt School of Engineering, with a minor in visual arts. This combination reflects my interdisciplinary approach to problem solving: I like to approach challenges from both an analytical and a creative perspective.

Mathematics is the analytical side, and it has become a common language for everything else I study. It makes new subjects easier to pick up, and I use its notation in my notes for every class. Writing a proof is one of my favorite ways to think, building an argument from the ground up the way engineering builds a device, only on paper. Art is where I experiment in a different way, with color and form, to express something rather than prove it.

These sides blend across all my work, especially in my labs. Shaping an organ model at Duke's SEAL Lab takes an eye for form as much as technical understanding, and analyzing cell images at an FSU microparticle lab meant seeing a pattern before I could quantify it. Having these perspectives lets me be rigorous about a problem while staying open to more than one way of solving it.

For Summer 2027, I'm looking for opportunities in healthcare innovation where I can keep exploring the intersection of engineering, quantitative work, and design.

Rollator Oxygen-Canister Mount

At Duke Hospital, walking a patient on oxygen took a second staff member just to manage the canister. Our team designed and prototyped a wheeled cage that mounts the canister to the rollator itself, freeing that second person. We validated it against seven design criteria, reduced the tipping angle by 6°, and earned a 4.3 out of 5 usability rating from 30 evaluators, then presented it at the Duke Foundry showcase.

The final prototype: a wheeled cage that holds the oxygen canister and attaches to a standard hospital rollator, shown on the test turntable.

Cell Image Segmentation Pipelines

For my Signals and Systems final project, I came back to the segmentation problem from my publication to understand why it worked. In Python, I built three pipelines: frequency-domain enhancement with a high-pass filter, convolution-based edge segmentation, and a combination of the two. Combining them sharpened faint edges in low-contrast images, but the final segmentation barely changed, which showed me that convolution alone was already capturing the boundaries that mattered.

A microscopy image of cells and its Fourier spectrum (top), then the edge map and segmented cells from the combined pipeline (bottom). Most of the image’s energy sits at low frequencies, while cell boundaries live in the high-frequency content that edge detection picks up. Adding frequency enhancement strengthened faint edges but changed the final segmentation only slightly.

Modeling an Engineered Probiotic

For BME 260, Modeling Molecular and Cellular Systems, our team of seven built a multi-scale model of the probiotic yeast S. boulardii engineered to break down PET microplastics in the gut. The cell displays PETase and MHETase, and a genetic circuit that senses TPA, one of PET’s breakdown products, turns up enzyme production once degradation starts. Comparing three PETase variants with Michaelis-Menten kinetics in Python, we found QM-PETase removed about 24% of PET in two weeks, with byproducts staying well below toxic levels.

Top: the engineered S. boulardii cell displays PETase and MHETase, which break PET into MHET and then TPA and ethylene glycol. Bottom: simulated gut PET over 400 hours from a 10 µM start. QM-PETase produced the largest drop (24.4% in two weeks), followed by FAST-PETase (20.7%), while wild-type IsPETase leveled off near 2.6%.

SuperComp

SuperComp pulls my own Oura, Peloton, and Apple Health data into one daily table, including over 1.19 million heart-rate samples and 3,151 workouts, and turns it into a readiness score and a recommended session. The model is rule-based on purpose: it weighs HRV against my personal baseline along with resting heart rate and sleep, and it scores training with heart-rate-based TRIMP so a run, a lift, and a ride compare fairly. When I backtested it on 386 training days, readiness didn’t predict how much work I did, so I reframed it as a guardrail and started logging decisions to collect the right labels before adding machine learning.

Ten weeks of my own data. Nightly HRV (top) is plotted against a 28-day personal baseline, with the shaded band marking my normal range. The model’s readiness score (middle) dips and recovers with HRV, and daily training load (bottom) is heart-rate-based TRIMP. Across 386 backtested training days, readiness did not predict workout output (Spearman ρ = −0.002), which supports using it as a guardrail rather than a predictor.

ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces March 2026

Engineered Microparticles Suggest Proteolysis as a Critical Prerequisite for Chromatin Clearance in Macrophage Phagosomes

View in ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces ↗

Masahiro Fukuda, Mary Clayton Soto, Jacob Perkins, Gabriela De Jesus, Sailesti Joshi, Zixiang Leonardo Liu, Tristan P. Driscoll, Yan Li, Yi Ren, Jingjiao Guan

My contribution to this paper was the image analysis. At an FSU microparticle lab, from May to August 2025, I developed a MATLAB pipeline to segment and classify fluorescence images of about 20,000 cells, producing a standardized dataset for the paper's analysis. The main challenge was keeping classification accurate across fluorescence channels that varied widely in intensity and contrast. I addressed it by tuning the convolution-based segmentation and making the thresholding respond dynamically to the properties of each image.
